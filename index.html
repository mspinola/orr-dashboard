Trades, not weight changes. A position's weight moves every day with its price. A change in its share count is a decision, so that is what is listed. Buying shares (opening or adding to a long, covering a short) is above zero; selling (trimming or closing a long, shorting) is below.

Valued at the later day's price, in dollars. The page gives prices in local currency (yen for Tokyo listings) and values in dollars, so each market's exchange rate comes from all of its rows together. Options use their own row's value per contract.

Creations and redemptions scale every position by the same ratio. When most positions move by one common ratio, that is shown as a flow and each trade is measured against the scaled position, so new money does not show as two hundred small buys.

What a snapshot is. The fund publishes one book per day, dated that morning. Two trades in the same name between snapshots net to one, and an intraday round trip is invisible.

How this page reads ORR

A discretionary long/short equity fund, from the book it publishes each day

Source. The fund's site lists its whole book every day and keeps no history, so this page saves each day's list and builds everything from the difference between one day and the next. Collection began in October 2026; nothing here reaches back before that except the Performance and S&P panels, which use the fund's published returns and its market price.

Long, short, net, gross. Each position is its market value as a share of net assets, as the fund reports it. Shorts are negative. Net is long plus short; gross is long minus short. Cash, currency balances and money-market funds are left out of all four.

Trades are share counts. A weight moves every day with price, so a trade here is a change in the number of shares held. When most positions change by one common ratio, that is new money or a redemption, shown as a flow, not as two hundred small trades.

Country is the company, not the listing. A Mexican airport group listed in New York counts as Mexico. Index ETFs are their own sector, since a short S&P 500 ETF is a hedge, not a view on a sector.

Net is not market exposure. The shorts are mostly broad index ETFs and the longs are single companies, many outside the US, so the fund can move with or against the S&P by more or less than its net suggests. The S&P panel measures what it has actually done.

Not shown. Anything bought and sold inside one day. Options are counted at their market value, which understates the exposure they carry. What each holding contributed to the day's return is not computed: it needs a price for every one of some 280 stocks in eight markets.
